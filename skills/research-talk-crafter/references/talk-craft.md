## 1. Structure and narrative

- **A talk is an advertisement for the paper, not the paper itself.** Aim to give listeners intuition and make them want to read the paper. Don't try to show everything you know. [SPJ] Dreyer goes further: the main goal is to leave people feeling good about you and your work. [Dreyer] Eisner: a talk only has to be clear and memorable, not a reference. [Eisner]
- **Have one main message and build the talk top-down from it.** Doumont: one message the audience should keep if they keep nothing else, stated early. [Doumont] Peyton Jones: one key idea, narrow and deep rather than wide and shallow. [SPJ] Hill allows 1–3 key points. [Hill] Eisner: 3 technical things to understand plus 3 takeaways. [Eisner] MIT faculty: know your three-sentence takeaway. [MIT-EECS]
- **Open with a promise and a hook.** Winston: open with an "empowerment promise," meaning what the audience will know by the end, and don't open with a joke. [Winston] Peyton Jones: you have about 2 minutes to hook them. Motivation gets ~20% of the talk and the key idea ~80%. [SPJ] Dreyer's 20-minute plan: ~8 min intro (general problem, then specific problem), ~11 min key ideas, ~1 min on what else is in the paper. [Dreyer]
- **Let the intro frame how to think about the problem.** Show the audience something they didn't know they didn't know. State goals, inputs, outputs and constraints early. Give the why before the what, so the story feels anticipated rather than surprising. [Fatahalian]
- **Don't open with an outline slide.** Peyton Jones, Ernst and Huang all agree. Instead, give a brief roadmap after the motivation and use section/signpost slides in longer talks. [SPJ][Ernst][Huang] Fatahalian likes section slides as re-entry points for listeners who drifted. [Fatahalian] Mild disagreement: Doumont and the Freiburg deck do include a preview/outline. [Doumont][Freiburg]
- **Lead with examples.** Peyton Jones: examples are the main vehicle, so when time is short, cut the general case and keep the examples. [SPJ] Eisner: use one running example throughout. [Eisner]
- **Linearize and chunk.** Dreyer: split the talk into "talklets" of 2–4 minutes, each ending with a summary and a transition. Within a slide, move from old information to new. [Dreyer] Eisner: use an "oh, just one more thing" structure. [Eisner]
- **Related work: present it as approaches to the framed problem, not as a list of citations.** Peyton Jones says skip the related-work slide, credit others as you go, and never disparage. [SPJ][Fatahalian] Wagen disagrees for external talks: spend 30–40% on background and history. [Wagen]
- **Repeat the key point.** Winston: say important things about three times, since roughly 20% of the audience is drifting at any moment. Use verbal punctuation and "fences" around ideas. [Winston] Doumont calls preview, transitions and review "effective redundancy." [Doumont]
- **End with contributions, not "Thank you" or "Questions?".** Winston, Ernst, Schulzrinne, Huang and Grauman all say this. Leave the contributions slide up during Q&A. [Winston][Ernst][Schulzrinne][Huang][Grauman] Fatahalian: end on the broader significance, not a list of obvious future work. [Fatahalian] Winston's spoken close is a salute to the audience, or a joke. [Winston]
- **Make the work memorable.** Winston's "star": a symbol, a slogan, a salient idea (his example of a salient idea is the "near miss"), a surprise, and a story. [Winston]

## 2. Slide design

- **Use message titles, not topic titles.** Doumont: each visual's title is a short sentence answering "so what." [Doumont] Alley's assertion-evidence slides: a sentence headline of at most 2 lines, with visual evidence in the body. [Alley] Fatahalian, Huang, Stein, Eisner, Wagen and MATS all agree (e.g., not "Results"). [Fatahalian][Huang][Stein][Eisner][Wagen][MATS] Fatahalian's check: read the titles in thumbnail view, and they should tell the whole story. [Fatahalian]
- **Evidence for assertion-evidence slides.** In a randomized study (n=110 engineering undergrads, 8-minute MRI lesson, identical narration), AE slides averaged ~21 words per slide vs ~42 for default slides. The AE group scored better on comprehension (d≈0.81), had fewer major misconceptions, did better on 1-week recall (d≈0.89), and reported lower mental effort. There was no difference on basic multiple-choice items. [Alley/IJEE 2013] Caveats: one topic, one site, no animation, self-reported load, and some internal inconsistencies in the paper. Separately, students who built AE slides understood their own material better. [Alley]
- **One message per slide.** Doumont: one message per slide, and one slide per message. [Doumont] Duarte's Glance Test: the audience should get the slide in ≤3 seconds. [Duarte] Also Hale, Dreyer and Stein. [Hale][Dreyer][Stein]
- **Few words; don't read your slides.** Winston: slides should be condiments to what you say, not the main event. Cut text, backgrounds, logos and clutter. [Winston] Reynolds: "slideuments" serve neither talk nor document, so give a separate handout. [Reynolds] Mayer's redundancy principle: printed text duplicating narration doesn't help learning. [Mayer]
- **Disagreement on sentences:** Alley and Doumont want a full-sentence headline. Huang, Grauman and Schulzrinne say to avoid full sentences in the body (one-liners, no periods). These are compatible: a sentence headline with sparse body text. Tufte, by contrast, wants full sentences and dense handouts instead of slides. [Tufte]
- **Signal-to-noise and visual consistency.** Doumont's three principles: adapt to the audience, maximize signal-to-noise, use effective redundancy. [Doumont] Eisner: keep a consistent visual language, with a few colors and fonts that each have a fixed meaning. [Eisner] Grauman: don't let text jump position between consecutive slides. [Grauman]
- **Pointing.** Winston and Huang say don't use the laser pointer; build arrows or boxes into the slide. Winston's reason is that turning away breaks contact with the audience. [Winston][Huang] Huang: dim everything else with a ~10%-transparency overlay. [Huang] Freiburg allows a laser pointer "sparingly." [Freiburg]
- **Tufte's critique.** Slides are low-resolution. Bullet outlines hide causal structure and fragment reasoning. A typical slide holds ~40 words, about 8 seconds of reading. Templates "corrupt" statistical analysis. For serious technical material, use dense handouts (e.g., 11×17 paper). [Tufte] He builds on the Columbia Accident Investigation Board's 2003 finding about NASA's reliance on PowerPoint. [Tufte] **Doumont's rebuttal:** slides are viewed *while the speaker talks*, so they have a different job from documents. Bad slides are the presenter's fault, not the tool's. Both agree slides make poor handouts. [Doumont via gasstation]
- **Prepare analog first.** Reynolds, Eisner and Doumont all say to storyboard on paper or a whiteboard before opening slideware. [Reynolds][Eisner][Doumont]

## 3. Figures and data (results slides)

- **Paper figures are not talk figures.** Split a dense figure across slides, show less data, and add circles, arrows or callouts. If the slide's point takes more than one sentence, it holds too much. [Stein] Fatahalian: one point per results slide, and that point is the title. Remake graphs from the paper and drop data that doesn't serve the point. [Fatahalian]
- **Explain every figure aloud.** Name the axes, say what each line or color is, then state the single point. [Fatahalian][Huang] Schulzrinne: label the axes and say the rough range out loud, because it is hard to read from the back. [Schulzrinne]
- **Never paste a paper table.** Highlight and interpret the key numbers; a citation tag like "[17]" means nothing to listeners. [Huang][Ernst][Hill] Freiburg prefers plots to tables. When you must use a table, use horizontal rules only, mark higher/lower-is-better with arrows, and highlight the best and second-best results. [Freiburg]
- **Plot hygiene (ML-specific).** Keep colors consistent for each method across *all* plots. Order the legend to match the curves. Use vector graphics. [Freiburg] Use large, simple bar charts with values printed on the bars, label the metric and which direction is better, and use about 3–5 colors. Show error bars: for proportions, SE = sqrt(p(1−p)/N), and when comparing conditions, compute the error bars on the *difference*. Show the raw ingredients (prompts, outputs). Keep a backup section for likely questions. [MATS]
- **Diagrams:** about 7 components is the most people can hold in mind. [Might] Use the simplest visual that makes the point. [Grauman]

## 4. Equations

- **Show the minimum, define every symbol, and favor intuition.** Peyton Jones: dense notation puts audiences to sleep, so point to the paper for detail. [SPJ] Schulzrinne: show the simplest form, more than ~3 terms wastes time, and define each symbol when you introduce it. [Schulzrinne] Might: introduce at most 4–7 symbols at a time and give each equation its own slide. [Might] Fatahalian: explain every equation. [Fatahalian]
- **Color-link terms** across equations, and between an equation and its diagram. [Grauman]
- Keep notation consistent across the talk. Put the full derivations and algorithms in backup slides, and teach the intuition with diagrams, animations or toy examples. [Freiburg]

## 5. Video and animation (robotics / ML)

- **Animate to build, not to decorate.** Build complex diagrams step by step with components aligned, so transitions don't add load. [Huang] Peyton Jones: use animation only very sparingly, for a punch line. [SPJ] Dreyer: build visuals incrementally with smooth animation, but don't reveal bullets one at a time. [Dreyer] Wagen: "appear" animations only. [Wagen] Eisner: use animation to show change. [Eisner]
- **Embed video files locally.** Don't link to YouTube; trigger play/stop with animation. [Huang] Test video playback on the actual machine and in the conferencing software, on both screens. [Freiburg]
- **Qualitative examples:** describe one or two in depth rather than flashing many. [Grauman]
- **Honesty conventions for robot footage.** Put the following *on the video itself* (not in a caption or description): the speed-up factor, the control mode (teleop / scripted / autonomous), any external localization (mocap), and tethers. [Hizook][RAS] Rosen et al.: flag edits and splices, label the playback rate (and show a real-time clip alongside sped-up ones), name supporting systems (QR tags, mocap, planners), record all trials, and say how many attempts the shown success took. [Rosen] RAS: show one uninterrupted take before close-ups, add ~15 s of failures or outtakes, keep videos 1–3 min, and caption them even when narrated. [RAS]
- **Show failures and limits.** Paxton's satire lists the misleading moves: cherry-picked scenes, weak baselines, hidden failures, and short clips. The honest version is to show diverse scenes and failure cases and to compare against strong baselines. [Paxton] Yan, for industry talks: tell the problem → what didn't work → what worked. [Yan]

## 6. Delivery and preparation

- **Enthusiasm is the biggest factor.** [SPJ] Winston: show passion. [Winston]
- **Rehearse aloud with a timer, and give practice talks.** The first run always runs long. [Eisner] Might rehearses about 10 times. [Might] Ernst: practice audience under 10 people, and record yourself. [Ernst] Fatahalian: if you keep forgetting what comes next, the structure is wrong. Put the start of each transition sentence in the previous slide's notes. [Fatahalian]
- **Memorize or script the opening lines** to get past nerves. [SPJ][Eisner] Speak from an outline, not a script, and use silence instead of filler words. [Doumont][MIT-EECS]
- **Finish on time, always.** If you're running long, truncate and conclude; don't ask to continue. [SPJ]
- **Questions:** repeat or rephrase the question, pause, say "I don't know" when that's true, and keep backup slides. [Ernst][Doumont][Freiburg] Fatahalian: the audience is always right about what was unclear. Ask what would have helped rather than defending the slide. [Fatahalian]
- **Don't apologize** (for preparation, equipment, time). [SPJ]
- **Room and timing:** Winston's ideal is late morning in a well-lit room, and check the venue beforehand. [Winston] Reynolds (citing Medina): change the format about every 10 minutes. [Reynolds]

## 7. Accessibility

- **Font size:** at least 18 pt, ideally 24 pt, including for code. [Deque] See the numbers table for others' minimums.
- **Contrast (WCAG):** at least 4.5:1 for normal text, and 3:1 for large text (≥18 pt, or ≥14 pt bold). Check with a contrast analyzer. [Deque]
- **Color vision:** about 8% of Caucasian males, 5% of Asian males and 4% of African males have red-green color vision deficiency. [Jfly] (Ernst says ~5% of American males.) Avoid red/green pairs. Use the Okabe–Ito palette (vermilion, bluish green, sky blue, orange, reddish purple, etc.). Never rely on color alone: add line styles, markers and direct labels instead of a separate legend. Use magenta/green for two-channel images. [Jfly][Eisner][Freiburg][Ernst]
- **Video and motion:** caption videos, even narrated ones. [RAS] Avoid flashing content (epilepsy risk), keep animations simple (vertigo), and keep loops/GIFs ≤5 s where possible. [Deque]
- **Verbal description:** describe meaningful visuals aloud, and speak at a pace captioners can follow. Share slides in advance. [Deque] This overlaps with "explain every figure." [Fatahalian]

## 8. Talk-type differences

- **Conference talk (12–20 min):** advertise the paper with one idea. Hill's 20-minute template is about a dozen slides: 1–2 motivation, 0–1 related work, 1 method, 4–6 results, 1 summary, 0–3 backup. [Hill][SPJ][Dreyer]
- **Job talk (~45–60 min slot):** talk for 35–40 min and leave room for Q&A. [MIT-EECS] Winston: within 5 minutes show that you have a vision and have done something, then list the steps toward the vision and your contributions. [Winston] Faculty advice: explain one main contribution fully, keep ~80–90% of the talk accessible to people outside your area with a few slides just for experts, and present a distinctive long-term research vision. [MIT-EECS] Hill: expand a conference talk's intro from 5 to 20 min, add 10 min of depth and 10 min surveying other work. [Hill]
- **Thesis defense:** open with motivation, then an active, declarative thesis statement. Restate the "contract" from the proposal and present the evidence. Put answers to likely critical questions in the main talk and the rest in backup. Unlike a conference talk, you can't defer questions to later. [Might]
- **Lab meeting / mentor update:** open with a summary of last meeting's takeaways and current status. Use a prioritized agenda with time per section, show experiment setup and raw outputs, and end with the decisions or input you need. Keep one living deck per project, newest slides first. [MATS] Ernst: research-group talks should be interactive. [Ernst] Fatahalian: polishing remade graphs is for conference talks, not informal lab talks. [Fatahalian]
- **External / cross-field talk:** pitch it at the adjacent-field majority, give 30–40% to background, teach something, and leave ≥10 min for Q&A. [Wagen]
- **Industry / technical meetup:** expect a tired audience; aim for 30–45 min with a problem → failures → what worked arc, and show bottom-line metrics. [Yan]
- **Pitch:** Kawasaki's rule is 10 slides, 20 minutes, ≥30 pt (or the oldest attendee's age ÷ 2); critics say treat it as a heuristic, not a law. [Kawasaki] Hale (YC): slides should be legible, simple and obvious, with one idea per slide, big bold high-contrast text, and the conclusion stated on the slide; Demo Day is about 5–7 slides in 2.5 min. [Hale]
- **Teaching / lecture:** Winston prefers the chalkboard for informing. Writing speed matches comprehension speed, and the board gives your hands something to do; slides are for exposing work. [Winston] Mayer's principles apply most directly here: segmenting (learner-paced chunks), pre-training (introduce key terms first), signaling, coherence (cut extraneous material), spatial and temporal contiguity, and modality (narration plus graphics beats on-screen text). [Mayer]

## Consensus rules (near-universal)

1. One main message per talk and one point per slide. Cut everything that doesn't serve it.
2. Slide titles state the takeaway (an assertion), not the topic.
3. Slides support the speaker: keep text minimal and never read slides aloud.
4. Redesign paper figures for the talk: less data, highlight the key element, explain the axes, and state the single point.
5. Explain intuition with examples; push technical detail and proofs to the paper or backup slides.
6. Motivate early (first ~2–5 min): why the problem matters and what is new.
7. End on contributions or the main message, not "Thank you" or "Questions?".
8. Use large fonts (≥20–24 pt), high contrast and sans-serif type, and never encode meaning by red/green alone.
9. Animation is for building up or showing change, not decoration.
10. Rehearse aloud with a timer and an audience, and always finish on time.
11. Be honest with results: show failures, and label speed-ups and autonomy on robot video.

## Disagreements and tensions

- **Should slides exist at all?** Tufte says use dense handouts and high-resolution evidence instead. Doumont, Alley and Reynolds say slides are fine if designed as visual support, with a separate document for detail.
- **Should slides stand alone?** Doumont wants each visual understandable without the speaker. Eisner wants slides readable online. Winston and Dreyer want slides that don't work without you (avoid "PowerPoint-itis"). Common compromise: two versions, one to present and one to hand out. [Winston]
- **Full sentences?** Alley and Doumont want sentence headlines, Tufte wants sentences everywhere, and Huang, Grauman and Schulzrinne want one-liners with no full sentences.
- **Outline slides:** Peyton Jones, Ernst and Huang say not at the start. Doumont, Hill and Freiburg include a preview or outline. Ernst endorses section-progress outlines only in talks over 30 min.
- **Related work:** Peyton Jones says no related-work slide. Hill allows 0–1. Wagen gives 30–40% to background in external talks. Fatahalian prefers framing related work as alternative approaches.
- **Slide pace:** 1 slide/min (Ernst, Freiburg, Eisner) vs 2 min/slide (Hill, Schulzrinne). Might and Winston favor many very light slides.
- **Font minimum:** ranges from 16 pt (Schulzrinne) to 40–50 pt (Winston).
- **Laser pointer:** Winston and Huang ban it. Freiburg allows it sparingly.
- **Slides vs board for teaching:** Winston favors the chalkboard for informing. Most CS advice assumes slides.
- **Goal of a conference talk:** "make them read the paper" (Peyton Jones, Ernst) vs "leave a positive impression of you and the work" (Dreyer) vs "teach something" (Wagen).
